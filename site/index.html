Who gets the loan, and what a model learns from who did.

In 1990 the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston gathered the loan files behind mortgage applications to study whether race was associated with denial. This audit reruns the textbook version of that data two ways: the econometrics of the approval gap, and what a model trained without race does by race. Results are associations after controlling for the variables recorded, not proof of discrimination or of its absence.

Read this first: what this can and can't show

The limits come before the results because they decide how the results can be read.

  • Associations, not causes. A gap that remains after the controls is consistent with discrimination. It is also consistent with things the files don't record, or record with error. No number here proves that any lender discriminated, or that none did.
  • The controls aren't neutral. Some recorded variables, like the lender's own "meets credit guidelines" judgment, were made by the people being studied. Controlling for them can hide part of a real gap. Leaving out true creditworthiness can make a gap look bigger than it is. The Econometrics tab shows how the answer moves with each choice.
  • The outcome is a decision, not a repayment. "Approved" records what a lender decided in 1990, not whether the borrower would have repaid. A model trained on it learns to imitate those lenders.
  • Small groups. There are Black or Hispanic applicants ( and ). Every estimate is shown with its 95% interval, and some of the intervals are wide.
  • One place, one year. This is a textbook subset of the Boston Fed's study, and the study's data quality was itself debated (see Method). Nothing here describes any lender today. Version 2 of this project will use current public HMDA data for Virginia.

A model that never sees race

Show every group, with AUC and accuracy

How to read the rates: the "truth" here is the lender's 1990 decision. TPR is the share of applicants the lenders approved whom the model also approves; FPR is the share the lenders denied whom the model approves. Equal TPRs is "equal opportunity"; equal TPRs and FPRs is "equalized odds"; equal approval rates is "demographic parity". Each one is measured against the 1990 decisions, which is the problem the label-bias section takes up.

Calibration: does the same score mean the same thing?

Observed approval rate against the model's average predicted probability, in fixed score bins, by group. Points on the diagonal are well calibrated. Bars are 95% intervals.
